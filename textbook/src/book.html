Big idea

The same scene can be understood in different ways depending on the perspective we choose. A perspective is like a lens: it makes some things clear and leaves others out of focus. In this unit you will learn to use four lenses — and to put them together.

Lesson 1

The Time Lens시간적 관점 — 시간의 흐름 속에서 현상을 보기

Goal Explain what the temporal perspective is, and use it to read a change over time.

Every social phenomenon has a history. A cheap T-shirt, a crowded subway, an empty classroom in a small town — none of them appeared overnight. When we look at the world through the time lens, we ask how things came to be the way they are. This is what social scientists call the temporal perspective시간적 관점: understanding a phenomenon by focusing on its historical background and the flow of time.

The time lens works in three directions. First, it looks backward, collecting facts and events from the past. Second, it connects the past to the present, so that we can explain why a problem exists today. Third, it looks forward: if a trend continues, what will the future look like? Because it links past, present, and future, the time lens helps us find causes and predict changes.

Key questions of the time lens
  • How has this phenomenon changed from the past to the present?과거부터 현재까지 어떻게 변화해 왔는가?
  • Why did the problem begin, and which past cases can help us solve it?왜 발생했고, 참고할 과거 사례는 무엇인가?
  • If this trend continues, how will our world change?이 추세라면 세계는 어떻게 변할 것인가?

Lesson 1 · The Time Lens시간적 관점
Reading 2

Why are the classrooms getting emptier?

Look again at Figure 1.1. In 1970, a Korean woman had four or five children on average. By 1990 the number had dropped below two, and in 2023 it reached 0.72 — the lowest figure ever recorded in the world. Why does this matter to you? Fewer births in the past mean fewer classmates today, and fewer workers, soldiers, and taxpayers tomorrow.

The time lens shows that today's "low birth rate problem" did not begin last year. It is the result of changes that started more than fifty years ago: rapid movement from farms to cities, longer education, later marriage, and the rising cost of housing and raising children. Together, over half a century, these choices produced the chart on the previous page.

Seeing the whole trend helps us understand the present without blaming any single generation, and it lets us look ahead. If the trend continues, Korea's population will shrink, and every young person will support more older people. That prediction is exactly why the issue is urgent now, not later.

{{svg:classroom-desks}}
Figure 1.2 Then and now: the number of elementary school students in Korea fell by more than half between 1980 and 2023. Source: Korean Educational Statistics Service (rounded).

Lesson 2

The Place Lens공간적 관점 — 장소, 분포, 연결망 속에서 현상을 보기

Goal Explain what the spatial perspective is, and use a map to trace a global connection.

Where something happens is never an accident. Farms grow where the soil and the rain are good. Factories cluster near ports and cheap labor. Cafés open next to subway exits. The place lens — the spatial perspective공간적 관점 — examines social phenomena by focusing on places, regions, and the interactions between them.

The place lens asks three kinds of questions. Location: where is it? Distribution: where is it concentrated, and where is it missing? Movement and networks: how do people, goods, money, and ideas travel from one place to another? "Space" here means both the natural environment — climate, mountains, rivers, resources — and the human environment that people have built: cities, roads, borders, and markets.

Key questions of the place lens
  • What are the characteristics of the space we live in?우리가 사는 공간의 특징은 무엇인가?
  • How do natural and human environments make life different in each region?환경에 따라 지역의 생활 모습은 어떻게 다른가?
  • What causes a place to change, and how does that change affect people's lives?공간 변화의 원인과 그 영향은 무엇인가?

Lesson 2 · The Place Lens공간적 관점
Map Lab

The journey of a smartphone

{{svg:map-phone}}
Figure 2.2 A simplified map of one phone's journey: raw materials, processing and assembly, and final markets. Arrows show the main flows, not every route.

A smartphone is a small map of the world. Its battery needs cobalt, and most of the world's cobalt is dug out of the ground in one country, the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Its lithium comes mainly from Australia and Chile. These metals are shipped to China, which refines them and assembles a large share of the world's phones; Vietnam and India are fast-growing assembly centers. The design, the brand, and most of the profit, however, stay in a few wealthy countries — including Korea and the United States.

When one link in this chain breaks — a mine closes, a border shuts, a government limits exports — prices rise everywhere. The place lens makes these hidden connections visible. It explains why a conflict in central Africa can change the price of a phone in Seoul.

Seoul, where the phone is designed, …

Lesson 3

The Rules Lens사회적 관점 — 사회 구조와 제도 속에서 현상을 보기

Goal Explain what the social perspective is, and show how an institution can change people's lives.

People do not live alone. We are born into families, go to schools, work in companies, and vote in elections. These repeated patterns of relationships form a social structure사회 구조: an organized framework that shapes how people relate to one another. Inside this structure are social institutions사회 제도 — established rules, practices, and procedures, such as laws, schools, elections, and markets, that guide people's behavior in a certain direction.

The rules lens — the social perspective사회적 관점 — studies a phenomenon by examining these structures and institutions. It looks for the problems inside the system and searches for solutions within the system: a new law, a better policy, a changed procedure. Individuals matter, of course. But the rules lens reminds us that many "personal" choices are shaped by the structures around us — and that changing a rule can change millions of lives at once.

Key questions of the rules lens
  • How do laws, institutions, and policies affect people's lives?법·제도·정책은 삶에 어떤 영향을 주는가?
  • How does social structure shape the way people think and act?사회 구조는 사고와 행동에 어떤 영향을 주는가?
  • What roles do government and citizens play in making policy?정책 결정에서 정부와 시민의 역할은 무엇인가?

Lesson 3 · The Rules Lens사회적 관점
Reading 2

Old enough to vote

In December 2019, the National Assembly changed the election law and lowered the voting age from 19 to 18. In April 2020, about 530,000 eighteen-year-olds voted for the first time. Nothing about these students had changed. The institution had.

The effects came quickly. Politicians suddenly had a reason to listen to teenagers. Schools began to teach about elections and political neutrality. Young people's opinions about tuition, housing, and the climate gained new weight, because those opinions could now become votes. The rules lens explains this change without looking at any single person: a law defined who counted as a full citizen, and changing that law changed people's lives.

The same lens also raises the next questions. Should 18-year-olds be allowed to run for office? Should political education be part of every school's curriculum? Who should decide — the government, the schools, or the students themselves? Each of these is a question about institutions, and each can be answered by changing them.

Another example · the plastic bag rule

From 2019, large stores in Korea were no longer allowed to give customers free single-use plastic bags. Within months, millions of shoppers were carrying their own bags. No campaign had changed their minds — a rule had changed their habits.

Language focus · rules, causes, and effects
The law requires / allows / bans X.
Because of the new rule, people …
The change led to a rise in …
As a result, schools began to …
  • require = must do; allow = may do; ban = must not do
  • lead to is followed by a noun: led to a change

Lesson 4

The Values Lens윤리적 관점 — 도덕적 가치와 규범으로 현상을 보기

Goal Explain what the ethical perspective is, and evaluate a phenomenon using moral values and norms.

Some questions cannot be answered with data alone. A chart can tell us how many animals are used in product tests every year. It cannot tell us whether that is acceptable. For questions like this we need the values lens — the ethical perspective윤리적 관점. It interprets social phenomena through moral values도덕적 가치, such as human dignity, freedom, fairness, and respect for life, and through moral norms도덕규범 — the shared standards that tell us what we ought to do.

The values lens does two jobs. First, it evaluates: is this situation good or bad, fair or unfair, worthy of a human being or not? Second, it proposes: what would a good and responsible way of living look like, and in which direction should society move? Without this lens, a society can be rich, efficient, and well organized — and still be wrong.

Key questions of the values lens
  • Judged by moral values and norms, is the current situation desirable?현재 상황은 도덕적으로 바람직한가?
  • Which universal values or principles should guide the solution?어떤 보편적 가치와 원칙으로 문제를 해결할 것인가?
  • When rights or interests conflict, which values should decide?이해관계나 권리가 충돌할 때 필요한 가치는 무엇인가?

Case

The rabbit and the lipstick

For decades, new cosmetics were tested on animals — for example, a substance was dropped into a rabbit's eye or rubbed onto its skin to see whether it caused irritation. Public opinion slowly changed. People argued that a new shade of lipstick was not worth an animal's suffering, and that companies could use alternatives such as artificial skin.

The European Union banned animal testing of cosmetics in 2013. In 2017, Korea amended its Cosmetics Act to ban the sale of cosmetics tested on animals, with limited exceptions. Today more than forty countries have similar rules.

{{svg:rabbit-cosmetics}}
Figure 4.1 A value ("respect for life") became a norm ("don't test beauty products on animals") and then a law.
Lesson 4 · The Values Lens윤리적 관점
Reading 2

Values first, rules second

Notice the order of events in the cosmetics case. First came a moral judgment: causing pain to an animal for a lipstick is wrong. Then came a norm that more and more people shared: beauty products should not be tested on animals. Only at the end came a rule — a law with a date and a penalty. The values lens often works this way. Values change first, and institutions follow.

But ethical questions are rarely simple. Is a test still wrong if it might save human lives, as in medicine? Who decides which value comes first when two of them conflict — the freedom of a business, or the welfare of an animal? Is it fair to ban a practice in Korea while buying products from countries that still allow it?

The values lens does not give easy answers. What it gives us is a way to argue about hard questions with reasons, not just feelings: naming the values at stake, weighing them, and proposing a direction that we could defend in front of anyone.

Lesson 5

The Integrative Lens통합적 관점 — 네 관점을 하나로 모아 세상을 보기

Goal Explain what the integrative perspective is and why it is necessary for understanding complex phenomena.
{{svg:four-rings}}
Figure 5.1 Each lens shows part of the picture. Only together do they show the whole.

Each lens shows something real, and each lens misses something. The time lens explains how a problem grew, but not where it hurts most. The place lens maps the problem, but not who is responsible. The rules lens finds the institutions, but not whether they are just. The values lens judges, but cannot, by itself, measure.

Modern problems — climate change, an aging population, artificial intelligence — are complex and uncertain because many factors interact across time and space. With only one lens, we may solve the wrong problem, or the right problem in the wrong way.

The integrative perspective통합적 관점 means examining humans, society, and the environment by considering all four together: historical background, spatial context, the influence of structures and institutions, and the direction given by moral values. It crosses the borders between subjects — history, geography, politics, ethics — to understand a phenomenon as a whole. That is why this subject is called Integrated Social Studies.

The Four Reporters

A heavy rain flooded the old part of a river town. Four reporters went to cover the story. The first checked the records. "The river has flooded five times in twenty years," she wrote, "and each flood was worse than the last." The second walked the streets with a map. "Only the low neighborhoods near the river went under water," he reported. "The new apartments on the hill stayed dry."

The third went to city hall. "A flood wall was planned in 2015," she found, "but the budget was cut, and no law forced the city to build it." The fourth talked to the families. "The people who lost their homes are the poorest in town," he wrote. "Is it right that those with the least always suffer the most?"

That evening the editor read all four stories. "Each of you is right," she said, "and each of you is only a quarter right. Put them together, and we will finally understand this flood — and maybe prevent the next one."

Time lens

How did it grow?

"Fast fashion" — cheap clothes in quickly changing styles — began in the 1990s and exploded with online shopping in the 2010s. Between 2000 and 2014 the amount of clothing produced in the world roughly doubled, while the number of times each item was worn before being thrown away fell by about a third.

{{chart:fashion}}
Figure 6.2 Two indexes (2000 = 100). Sources: McKinsey & Company (2016); Ellen MacArthur Foundation (2017).
Place lens

Where is it made, worn, and dumped?

Fast fashion is a global network. Clothes are designed in Europe, the United States, and Korea; sewn mostly in Bangladesh, Vietnam, Cambodia, and China; and worn everywhere. When they are thrown away, many are shipped abroad again. Reporters have photographed mountains of discarded clothing in Chile's Atacama Desert and in the markets of Accra, Ghana — thousands of kilometers from the closets they came from.

The place lens shows an unequal map: the benefits (cheap clothes, brand profits) and the costs (low wages, pollution, waste) fall on very different places.

Rules lens

Which institutions shape it?

On April 24, 2013, the Rana Plaza factory building in Bangladesh collapsed, killing more than 1,100 garment workers. Cracks had been reported the day before, but workers were ordered back inside. After the disaster, global brands signed a legally binding building-safety agreement, and inspections became routine.

Governments are acting, too. France banned companies from destroying unsold clothing (in force since 2022), and the European Union announced a strategy to make textiles last longer and be easier to recycle. Rules like these change what companies find profitable.

Values lens

Is it right?

Is it fair that a worker who sews a shirt earns a tiny fraction of its price? Do we owe anything to the people who will live with our waste for centuries? Is "I can afford it" a good enough reason to buy it?

Values such as fairness, the dignity of labor, and moderation lead many people to a different norm: buy less, buy better, wear longer. Whether that norm becomes a rule is a question for the whole society.

Putting it together

Seen through all four lenses, fast fashion is not just a shopping habit. It is a thirty-year trend time, a global network of unequal places place, a system of rules that can be rewritten rules, and a moral question about fairness and responsibility values. A response that uses only one lens — for example, only telling shoppers to feel guilty — will fail. An integrative response combines better rules, fairer trade, and changed habits.

The midnight game ban게임 셧다운제를 네 가지 관점으로 토론하기

From 2011 to 2021, Korea had a "shutdown law." Under this rule, children under sixteen could not log in to online games between midnight and six in the morning. Lawmakers and many parents said it protected sleep, health, and study. Young people and game companies argued that it was ineffective and unfair: it did not apply to mobile games, teenagers borrowed their parents' accounts, and it treated all families the same.

In 2022 the law was abolished. Families may now set their own limits through a "game-time choice" system. The debate did not end, however. Around the world, the same questions are now being asked about smartphones and social media — in 2024, for example, Australia passed a law setting a minimum age of sixteen for social media accounts.

Four lenses, one world빈칸을 채우며 정리하기

Time lens
시간적 관점
Examines a phenomenon by focusing on its ① background and the flow of time. Collects facts from the past, links past and present, and ② future change.
Place lens
공간적 관점
Focuses on places, regions, and spatial interaction — location, ③, movement, and networks — in both the natural and the human environment.
Rules lens
사회적 관점
Examines social ④ and social institutions such as laws and policies; identifies problems in the system and seeks solutions within it.
Values lens
윤리적 관점
Interprets phenomena through moral values and moral ⑤; evaluates whether a situation is desirable and proposes a good direction for society.
Integrative lens
통합적 관점
Considers all four together to understand humans, society, and the environment as a ⑥. Necessary because modern phenomena are ⑦ and uncertain, and one lens alone may miss important aspects.
